# Blind check: why did prices stay high?

13 games from Experiment 3 ended with a collusion index of 0.25 or more (rounds 151–200).
They are shown here as Games 1 to 13, in random order, with the model names removed.

Each folder has:

- `prices.png`: both firms' prices over the game (shaded = rounds 151–200)
- `rounds.csv`: every round's prices, quantities and profits
- `notes.txt`: every answer from both firms, round by round (large file: click "Raw" on GitHub, then Ctrl+F / Cmd+F)

## Question 1: what mainly kept prices high? (pick one)

| Answer | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Stuck** | Firms stop testing other prices and sit still. Their notes call the price "optimal", "validated" or an "equilibrium", without reasoning about how the rival would react. |
| **Fear of retaliation** | Firms keep prices high because they expect a cut to be matched or punished ("price war", "they'd follow", "retaliation"). |
| **Price leader and follower** | One firm sits higher on purpose because the other firm's price follows its own; the other firm just picks its best price against the first. Usually one flat high line and one lower line. |
| **Still probing** | Firms keep changing prices up and down late in the game, testing how the rival reacts. |
| **Other / unclear** | Something else, or not enough evidence. Explain in "Why". |

## Question 2: when a firm cut its price, did the rival cut too?

Look at `rounds.csv` or the chart: after a firm dropped its price below the rival's,
did the rival's price fall within the next round or two?

- **Yes:** this happened at least a few times
- **No:** cuts were not followed, or no firm ever cut
- **Not sure**

Question 1 is about the firms' reasons. Question 2 is about what actually happened.
A firm can fear retaliation (Q1) that never actually happens (Q2 = No).
